Grass Holds the Yellow River

The Yangtze, Yellow, and Lancang rivers all begin on the same plateau.

With an average elevation above 4,700 meters, this is one of Asia's most important water-source regions. Meltwater, precipitation, and groundwater converge into rivers that sustain hundreds of millions of people downstream.

Yet snow-capped mountains are not the only things that help this plateau retain water. So does a layer of grass only a few centimeters tall.

This is the story of life that no one tracks, that draws no attention, and seldom appears on conservation posters. Hidden in the soil and pressed close to the riverbed, these organisms quietly determine how long a river can keep flowing.

A Living Turf Mat Stores the Water

Above ground, the alpine meadows of the Tibetan Plateau appear unremarkable.

The dominant Kobresia species belong to the sedge family. Their leaves grow close to the ground, usually reaching only a few to a dozen centimeters in height. The most important part lies underground.

Their roots interweave through the soil to form a dense "turf mat," opening countless tiny pores. When the snow melts, the water does not immediately run off. These pores retain it little by little, like an immense natural sponge.

This hidden structure sustains the plateau's wetlands and rivers. But when the meadow degrades, roots diminish and soil structure breaks down. The pores that once stored water disappear, runoff accelerates, and less water reaches the rivers.

Moderately degraded meadow soils store approximately 9% to 10% less water than intact meadow soils; severely degraded soils store 18% to 28% less.

Protecting the plateau meadow, then, means protecting more than grass. It means protecting an invisible high-altitude reservoir.

Only Six Fish Species Live at the Yangtze Headwaters

The sixth was not recorded until 2023.

An expedition team collected specimens of Triplophysa stolickai in the Dam Chu, the southern source of the Yangtze. Once confirmed, the number of known fish species in the southern headwaters rose from five to six.

Triplophysa is the only fish genus in the world found above 5,000 meters. Together with schizothoracine fishes, these loaches make up the dominant fish fauna of rivers across the Tibetan Plateau.

They are inconspicuous: typically only a dozen or so centimeters long, scaleless, with mouths on the underside of their bodies and mottled gray-brown coloration. Resting on a gravel riverbed, they nearly disappear among the stones.

No fur, no large eyes that lodge instantly in the memory. But it is here.
